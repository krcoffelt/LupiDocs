How much does it cost to self-publish a book?

Publishing platforms can make a book available with little or no upfront platform fee. Producing a book readers trust is a different question. Your real budget depends on the manuscript, formats, professional help, distribution choices, and launch plan.

By Christine Leninger · Reviewed August 3, 2026

Start with the two kinds of cost

First are platform and production costs: ISBN choices, proof copies, printing, distribution, and file changes. Second are professional service costs: editing, cover design, interior layout, project guidance, and marketing. A do-it-yourself author can reduce service spending, but the tradeoff is time, learning, and quality control.

Amazon KDP currently provides free tools for uploading print and ebook files and uses print-on-demand, so authors do not have to buy inventory in advance. Printing costs are deducted from print royalties. That makes “free to upload” accurate; it does not make the entire publishing process free.

The seven budget categories

1. Manuscript development

Book coaching, developmental editing, or a manuscript review helps solve structure, audience, and argument problems before polishing begins.

2. Editing and proofreading

Line or copy editing improves the prose; proofreading catches errors after layout. The length and condition of the manuscript affect the scope.

3. Cover and interior

A print cover must account for trim size, spine width, and printer specifications. Interior layout differs for print, fixed pages, and reflowable ebooks.

4. ISBN and imprint

KDP offers a free ISBN for eligible print books, but it identifies the imprint as “Independently published” and is limited to KDP. Buying your own ISBN gives you more control over the imprint and where that edition is used.

5. Proofs and printing

Page count, trim size, ink, paper, bleed, and marketplace affect print cost. Budget for physical proofs and use each platform’s current calculator before setting a list price.

6. Distribution and revisions

Retail reach, wholesale settings, returns decisions, and post-publication file changes can affect both cost and risk. Check current provider terms before committing.

7. Marketing and launch

Possible costs include an author site, email tools, advance copies, review outreach, launch assets, publicity, events, and advertising. Marketing spend should follow clear positioning—not compensate for an unfinished book or unclear reader promise.

Build the budget in the right order

  1. 1. Define the book. Confirm audience, purpose, word count, formats, and publication goal.
  2. 2. Assess the manuscript. Determine which editorial stage is actually needed.
  3. 3. Price the edition. Choose trim, paper, ink, page count, ISBN approach, and distribution path.
  4. 4. Protect the essentials. Prioritize editorial quality, readable files, and a credible cover before optional launch extras.
  5. 5. Keep a contingency. Proof corrections, extra rounds, shipping, and late changes are common.

A better question than “What is the average?”

Ask: what does this specific book need to reach its intended reader in a professional form? A short, text-only business book and an illustrated children’s book do not have the same production requirements. A polished manuscript and an early draft do not need the same editorial scope.

Get written proposals that identify deliverables, revision limits, responsibilities, and what is excluded. Avoid any provider that promises guaranteed sales, reviews, or bestseller status as the return on a package.
